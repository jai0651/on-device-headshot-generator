Then: choose the digit (class-conditional retrain)

The same UNet, retrained from scratch with a label embedding (digits 0–9 plus "any digit") added to the timestep embedding, and the label dropped 10% of the time. That's Stable Diffusion's text conditioning and classifier-free guidance in miniature. 10 epochs on an RTX 2000 Ada (~$0.08). A separately trained classifier (98.6% on MNIST) checks the output: 80 of 80 generated digits are the digit asked for. Command: uv run python -m stage0_ddpm.draw 7.

The headline, and the opposite of what the study notes expected

By the independent metric, zero-shot FaceID from one selfie (0.67) beats the trained LoRA (0.49) on headshots, with no per-person training and no photo upload, which is the property the on-device product needs. It also carries the face into other styles (0.70 vs 0.17). With 17 photos it reaches 0.77, and 0.82 with pose control.

faceid_1photo: the same person in all 24 images, including the astronaut and the painting. Note how many came out black-and-white.

lora: closer on some headshots, drifts on others, and still hides the face in the astronaut helmet.

What it costs, and what isn't settled

  • Prompt and colour adherence. FaceID images often come out desaturated or black-and-white unprompted; flexibility CLIP drops from 27.0 (base) to 23.9 with 17 photos. The face tokens partly override the prompt's style. Scale 0.6 recovers a little (24.5) for a little identity.
  • Is the metric fooled? Both recognizers are ArcFace-family networks, so FaceID could be making a face ArcFace-style models like rather than one people recognise. One check argues against it: FaceID's primary scores (the recognizer it was built on) are no higher than its secondary ones (0.65 vs 0.67). The real test is a person who knows the face, so there's a blind A/B test, outputs/stage5/blind_test.jpg, pending.
  • Stacking. LoRA + FaceID (0.78) is no better than FaceID alone (0.77) and follows prompts a bit better. They don't fight, but the LoRA adds little on top.
  • Pose control fixes framing, not compliance. It adds 1–1.7 passing checks for every method, but still 0 of the passport images pass all 13: background, lighting and sharpness remain. That's the job of the edit path (stage 5c).
  • Licensing. FaceID is non-commercial (built on insightface). The architecture works; a product needs it retrained on a commercially licensed face encoder.

Stage 5c: the edit path, and the FaceID colour fix

Instead of generating a face, keep the real one: reframe a selfie to passport geometry, keep the face and hair pixels, and let SD 1.5's inpainting model (with the passport pose ControlNet) paint clothes and backdrop around them. Optionally, a low-strength img2img pass over the whole image ("harmonize") to match the face's lighting to the new scene. 3 selfies × 3 outfits × 3 seeds, one RTX 3090 session (~$0.07). Three face crops were uploaded for this; no other photos.

8 of 27 edit-path images pass every one of the 13 ID-photo checks; every generated method, including FaceID with pose control, scored 0. Identity is 0.85–0.91, above any generated face, because it's the real face. This is the design the system-design page proposed, now measured.

Edit path, no harmonize: three selfies (rows of 9), blazer / suit / ID-photo outfits, three seeds each.

  • What still fails: background uniformity (12 of 27), uneven face lighting carried over from the selfie (12), sharpness (6), crown margin (3). The lighting failures are the selfie's own light; harmonize is meant to fix them, but it costs identity fast (0.89 → 0.81 → 0.69).
  • A bug found by looking: two ID-photo images got garbled text painted on the t-shirt. Fix: "text, letters, logo, watermark" in the negative prompt.
  • The trade-off vs generation: the edit path can't change the angle, expression or hairstyle: you get your selfie's pose. Generation (FaceID) can, at lower identity. A product offers both.

FaceID colour fix

Adding "black and white, monochrome, grayscale, desaturated" to the negative prompt took FaceID's near-greyscale images from 6 of 24 to 0 of 24, with identity unchanged (0.67 → 0.68) and passport compliance up (9.3 → 10.0). Mean saturation doubled (53 → 106), above the LoRA's 65: vivid, slightly punchy. The astronaut now shows the face.

Stage 6: Core ML, on the Neural Engine

Everything on the Mac, no GPU pod. The face LoRA was folded into the UNet weights (exact to 4.7×10⁻⁶), the UNet converted to Core ML FP16 with Apple's converter (77 s, 8 GB peak RAM), and the text encoder and VAE reused from Apple's official conversion, since the LoRA never touched them.

Same seed, same Core ML UNet. Pixel-wise they differ (18.8 dB), but it's the same person, pose and composition.

  • The face survives conversion, and the Neural Engine costs ~0.02 identity.
  • 100% of the UNet's 3,057 ops run on the Neural Engine, with zero device switches. VAE decoder: 397/397. Text encoder: 399/408 (9 token-bookkeeping ops on CPU).
  • Every network wants the Neural Engine on this chip: the VAE decoder is 2.8× faster there than on the GPU (0.23 vs 0.64 s). End to end ≈ 9.5 s per 512² image at 25 steps, before any quantization or LCM.
  • ANE drift is a different sample, not a worse one: 36 dB per step compounds into a different image of the same person. So ANE output is validated with scores, never by pixel-diffing against a GPU render.

Pose control on the Neural Engine: the head-cropping fix

Stage 7: quantization, how small before it stops being you

The phone path's UNet (face folded in, pose inputs) palettized after training to 8, 6 and 4 bits: each layer's weights clustered into 256 / 64 / 16 values. No retraining. All on the Mac.

6-bit647 MB15.6 s0.4621.3 dB24.211.4
4-bit432 MB14.8 s0.3312.2 dB23.911.8

Same seed, same prompts. 8-bit is the same image; 6-bit shifts details; 4-bit is noisy and harsh.

  • 8-bit is free: half the size, no measurable loss. The shipping default.
  • Identity breaks before "quality" does: at 4-bit, identity falls to the same-person line while CLIP and the compliance count barely move. Open question 1, answered.
  • A harness gap, closed: CLIP and compliance can't see texture artifacts. Image PSNR against the FP16 output at the same seed can, and now goes into every compression comparison.

Stage 8: LCM, 4 steps instead of 25

The LCM-LoRA distils SD 1.5 so each step jumps toward the finished image: 4 steps, no CFG, one UNet call per step. Folded into the UNet next to the face LoRA, single-pass, 8-bit, on the Neural Engine.

PyTorch comparison, same seeds: 25-step baseline (top), LCM 8 (middle), LCM 4 (bottom).

  • 12.6× faster and more like you. Open question 4 answered the opposite way to the guess: distillation raised identity (PyTorch: 0.45 → 0.63) at unchanged prompt adherence.
  • Why: generated faces became as consistent with each other (0.73–0.76) as real photos of you are (0.73). The guidance-6 baseline was the inconsistent one (0.43), pulled toward a generic face.
  • The cost: less variety of expression and angle, a smoother, more airbrushed look.
  • Sustained: 50 in a row, median 1.30 → 1.50 s, no thermal warning. The iPhone, with far less cooling, is still the number that matters.
