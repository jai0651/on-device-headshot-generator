◆System design — what I'm building and how it works

The rest of this document is the theory, one mechanism at a time. This section is the whole machine: what the product does, which problems it exists to solve, and how a selfie turns into a headshot without the photo ever leaving the phone.

What it does

You take a selfie, or pick one or more photos from your library. A few seconds later you get professional headshots: a clean studio backdrop, a blazer instead of a hoodie, soft even lighting, correct head-and-shoulders framing. The face has to be recognisably you. Everything runs on the iPhone, and nothing is uploaded.

Two halves: build time and run time

The system splits cleanly in two. Build time happens on rented cloud GPUs. It's where models are chosen, distilled, converted, quantized and measured, and it never sees a user's photo. Its only output is a model bundle. Run time happens on the user's phone, offline, using that bundle.

What happens on the phone, step by step

  1. Quality gate. Reject bad input before spending any compute: exactly one face, sharp enough (variance of the Laplacian), eyes open, not heavily backlit. A bad selfie can only produce a bad headshot, so this is the cheapest quality win in the system.
  2. Face analysis, once. Landmarks (for framing and compliance), a face-and-hair mask (for the edit path), and a face-recognition embedding (for the generate path and the identity guard). These are small models, and Apple's Vision framework covers most of it.
  3. Path A · Edit (the default). The face and hair pixels are kept as they are. Everything else (backdrop, clothes, shoulders) is inpainted, with ControlNet holding the framing. A low-strength img2img pass over the whole image then matches the lighting on the face to the new scene. Identity is preserved by construction, and low strength runs only a fraction of the denoising steps.
  4. Path B · Generate. For a new angle, expression or style. A style preset supplies the prompt, FaceID supplies who, ControlNet supplies where, and a 4–8 step LCM sampler at CFG 1 does the denoising, with the UNet on the Neural Engine.
  5. Decode and upscale. The VAE decoder turns the latent into 512² pixels (probably on the GPU, which section 05 says to measure), then a small upscaler doubles it.
  6. Identity guard and compliance. Score the result against the input with a different face recognizer from the one path B conditioned on, so the check isn't circular. Run the compliance checks from section 04. Below threshold: a new seed, or fall back to path A. The user never sees the failures.
A cheap win hiding in the design

The prompts are fixed style presets ("studio grey, navy blazer", "outdoor, soft daylight"), not free text. So their 77×768 text embeddings can be computed once at build time and shipped. That's about 118 KB each in fp16, instead of shipping and running a 123M-parameter text encoder on the phone. One network fewer to convert, quantize and hold in memory.

What running on rented GPUs taught me

Three pod sessions so far, about $0.35 in total. They surfaced these problems, all now handled in the scripts:

  • The stock API overstates cheap capacity. Community Cloud showed "Low" stock for $0.16–0.22 GPUs that couldn't actually be created, and spot pods are no longer offered at all. A create attempt is the only real availability check, so a retry loop now walks the approved GPUs cheapest first.
  • A pod can take ~8 minutes to boot while it pulls the image. The driver now waits 15 minutes before giving up.
  • The RunPod PyTorch image has no torchvision. The job script now installs the build that exactly matches the image's torch and CUDA, and imports it before doing any GPU work.
  • The remote launcher held the SSH session open (inherited stdin), which hung the automation. Fixed with setsid … </dev/null.
  • CPU work doesn't belong on a GPU pod. One pod reported 48 CPUs but was capped at 5.1; CPU-only identity validation started 288 threads and starved the GPU. The rule since: GPU work on the pod, CPU work (face recognition, compliance, scoring) on the Mac.
  • cd X && setsid job & holds the ssh session open until the job ends, because bash backgrounds the whole list in a subshell. Launch with ;, not &&.
  • Pod-to-laptop bandwidth was ~8 KB/s once, and ~2.4 MB/s another time. A 104 MB checkpoint would have taken hours of billed time. Jobs should write small artifacts (images, CSVs, logs) and only keep the weights that the next stage needs.
What worked

The self-delete switch did its job: the pod deleted itself 10 minutes after the job finished, with no one needing to remember. Running SD 1.5 alongside the small MNIST training kept the GPU busy instead of idle. And nothing ran on the laptop beyond short smoke tests.

00The one hard problem

Stable Diffusion has never seen my face.

That's it. That's the whole problem, and every technique below is a consequence of it. I can ask for a professional headshot and get a beautiful one — of a stranger. There's no token in the model's vocabulary that means me, there are no weights encoding my jaw, and no amount of prompt engineering will conjure them. Describing myself in words gets me somebody who matches the description, which is not the same thing at all.

So the project splits into two halves that fail for completely different reasons:

Half one — identity

Teach a general model one specific face, well enough that people who know me recognise it instantly, while keeping its ability to put that face in a suit, in new lighting, at a new angle. Fails by producing someone who merely resembles me, or by memorising my selfies so hard it can't do anything else.

Half two — the device

Take ~2 GB of float32 PyTorch and get it running inside an iPhone's memory budget at a tolerable speed, without wrecking the identity I just spent half one building. Fails by not converting at all, by silently falling off the Neural Engine, or by getting killed for peak memory.

What I want out of this document is to understand the mechanisms well enough that when something looks wrong I know which of maybe four things it could be, instead of changing hyperparameters at random and hoping.

01How diffusion actually makes a picture

The core trick is almost stupid when you first see it, and then you realise why it works.

Forward: take a real image and add a little Gaussian noise. Then a little more. Do it a thousand times and you have pure static. This direction needs no learning at all — it's just addition, and there's a closed form that jumps straight to step t without looping.

Reverse: train a network to undo one step. Give it a noisy image and the timestep, ask it to predict the noise that was added, subtract, repeat. Do that from pure static and a picture falls out of it.

Why does that produce anything coherent? Because at high noise levels the network can only make coarse decisions — is this a face or a landscape, where's the bright region — and at low noise levels it's only deciding on eyelashes. The iteration is what buys structure. One shot from noise to image would have to commit to everything at once, and that's a much harder function to learn.

What it actually predicts

Almost always the noise, not the image. Predicting ε keeps the target at roughly unit variance across every timestep, so the loss doesn't need reweighting. Some models predict v instead, which is a blend of noise and signal that behaves better at the extreme ends of the schedule. The training loss is just MSE between predicted and actual noise. There's no adversarial game, no discriminator, nothing unstable. That's a large part of why diffusion won.

Latents, not pixels

Running that loop on 512×512×3 = 786,432 numbers is brutally expensive. Stable Diffusion doesn't. It trains an autoencoder first, compresses the image to a 64×64×4 latent (16,384 numbers, 48× fewer), and runs the entire diffusion process in there. At the end, one pass of a decoder turns the latent back into pixels.

That single decision is what made this runnable on consumer hardware, and it's why "Stable Diffusion" is really three separate networks — which matters enormously for me later, because I convert, quantize and profile each one independently.

Samplers: why 50 steps became 20

Here's the thing that confused me for a while. The sampler is not part of the model. The UNet just predicts noise at a given timestep. Turning those predictions into a trajectory from static to image is a numerical integration problem, and you can swap the integrator without retraining anything.

  • DDPM — the original, stochastic, needs hundreds of steps.
  • DDIM — makes it deterministic and lets you skip timesteps. Same model, 50 steps instead of 1000.
  • DPM-Solver++ / UniPC — higher-order ODE solvers. Good images at 20–25 steps.

That's free speed from better numerics, and it's completely separate from the distillation work in section 06, which changes the model itself.

Classifier-free guidance, and why it doubles my bill

A raw conditional diffusion model follows the prompt weakly. CFG is the fix, and it's the reason "runs 2× per step" is in that table.

Each step you run the UNet twice — once with the prompt, once with an empty prompt — and then extrapolate away from the unconditional prediction:

noise = uncond + scale * (cond - uncond)

At scale = 1 you get the plain conditional output. Crank it to 7.5 and the model pushes hard toward whatever makes this prompt different from no prompt at all. Push much past that and faces go saturated and plastic, contrast blows out, and detail turns to wax.

The deployment consequence

20 sampler steps at CFG means 40 UNet forward passes, not 20. Any latency estimate that forgets this is off by 2×. There are tricks to avoid it — batching both passes together, or distilled models that bake the guidance in — and I'll need one of them.

02Four ways to teach it my face

These are genuinely different mechanisms, not variations on one. They differ in what they change, how big the artifact is, how long training takes, and how well the likeness holds. Understanding why each exists is most of the battle.

1 — Textual Inversion: invent a word

Freeze the entire model. Add one new vector to the text encoder's embedding table, call it <jai>, and optimise only that vector so that generating from prompts containing it reproduces my photos.

The artifact is a few kilobytes — literally one 768-dim vector. It composes beautifully with anything. But you're trying to describe a face using only the expressive range the frozen model already has, and one vector isn't much of a budget. Likeness comes out weak. Good for styles, not great for people.

2 — DreamBooth: retrain the whole thing

Fine-tune all the UNet weights on 15–20 photos, binding me to a rare token. Works very well and produces the strongest likeness of the classic methods.

Two problems. The artifact is a full model — gigabytes per person, which kills the "ship an adapter to the phone" architecture instantly. And it suffers language drift: train hard on photos of one man and the model quietly forgets what other men look like, so now every "person" it generates drifts toward me. The paper's fix is a prior preservation loss — generate a few hundred images of the generic class "a photo of a person" using the untouched model first, and train on those alongside my photos so the model is continuously reminded what the class looks like.

3 — LoRA: the one I'll actually use

This is the important one, so let me do the arithmetic properly.

Fine-tuning updates a weight matrix W to W + ΔW. The LoRA observation is that for a specific adaptation, ΔW has low intrinsic rank — the update isn't using anything like the full dimensionality available to it. So don't store ΔW as a dense d×k matrix. Store it factored:

ΔW = B · A      B is d×r,  A is r×k,  r ≪ min(d,k)

Train A and B, leave W frozen. For a 768×768 attention projection, that's 589,824 parameters down to 768×8 + 8×768 = 12,288 at rank 8. A 48× reduction. Applied across the UNet's attention blocks, my per-person artifact is somewhere around 10–60 MB instead of 3.4 GB.

Two details that make it better than the headline:

  • B is initialised to zero. So BA = 0 at step 0 and the adapted model is exactly the base model. Training starts from known-good rather than from a random perturbation, which is a big part of why LoRA is stable at learning rates that would wreck a full fine-tune.
  • It folds away at inference. Compute W + BA once, write it into the weights, and you're running an ordinary UNet. Zero runtime overhead, zero extra ops for the Core ML converter to choke on. For on-device work this is enormous — the adapter never has to exist on the phone as a separate thing.

Where it attaches

The cross-attention blocks, mainly — that's where text conditioning enters the UNet, so it's the natural lever for "make this prompt mean this face". Most implementations hit the to_q, to_k, to_v and to_out projections of both self- and cross-attention. Including the feed-forward layers helps a little and costs size.

Rank and alpha

Rank is capacity. Too low and it can't hold a face; too high and it memorises backgrounds and overfits like a full fine-tune. Alpha is a scaling factor applied as alpha/r — it exists so that changing rank doesn't force you to re-tune the learning rate. People treat it as a second knob but it mostly just trades off against LR.

4 — IP-Adapter: don't train at all

Completely different idea. The UNet already has cross-attention layers that attend to text embeddings. IP-Adapter bolts on a parallel set of cross-attention layers that attend to an image embedding, and sums the two outputs.

The word that matters is decoupled. The obvious approach — concatenate the image embedding onto the text sequence and use the existing attention — makes them compete for the same K/V projections, and the image tends to lose. Giving the image its own projections lets both signals apply at full strength.

Nothing in the base model changes. The adapter is ~22M params, trained once, on a big dataset, and then it works on any face at inference with zero training. One photo, instant results.

The catch, and it's the key insight of this whole section

Plain IP-Adapter uses a CLIP image encoder. CLIP was trained to match images with captions, so its embedding encodes "a man in his late twenties, short dark hair, brown skin". Feed that in and you get someone who matches that description — you get my cousin, not me.

ArcFace and friends are trained with a completely different objective: push two photos of the same person together in embedding space and two photos of different people apart, and nothing else. That's a far sharper notion of identity. IP-Adapter-FaceID, InstantID and PhotoMaker all essentially swap the generic image encoder for a face-recognition one. That swap is the difference between "resembles" and "is".

And separately: ControlNet, which is not about identity

Worth keeping in a different mental box. ControlNet clones the UNet's encoder into a trainable copy connected by zero-initialised convolutions, and conditions on a spatial map — pose skeleton, depth, edges, segmentation. It controls where things are, not who they are.

For headshots I need it anyway: framing, head position, shoulder angle and camera distance all have to be controllable if I'm going to hit a compliance spec. Identity and layout are orthogonal axes and I should be able to move them independently.

03Making it actually good

Getting a LoRA to train is easy. Getting one that produces a likeness people recognise and still does what the prompt asks is where all the difficulty lives. This section is the practical core.

The dataset is most of the quality

More than rank, more than learning rate, more than which base model. 15–25 images is the sweet spot — fewer and it can't generalise, more and you're mostly adding redundancy and training time.

What to include

  • Varied backgrounds. If every photo is my bedroom wall, the model learns that the wall is part of me.
  • Varied lighting — window, indoor, outdoor, overcast.
  • Varied angles — front, three-quarter both sides, slight up and down.
  • Varied expressions and a couple with and without a smile.
  • Varied distance — mostly head-and-shoulders, a few closer, a couple further out.
  • Sharp focus on the face. Resolution above 512 so cropping has room.

What to throw out

  • Other people in frame — a hard no, the model can't tell which face it's learning.
  • Sunglasses, hats, heavy shadow across the face.
  • Anything blurry or heavily compressed.
  • Photos from the same 10-second burst — that's one photo wearing four hats.
  • Strong filters or phone beauty-mode smoothing, which bakes in an effect.
  • Extreme wide-angle selfies — the lens distorts the nose and you'll learn the distortion.
The captioning rule that changes everything

Here's the principle I'd not have guessed: caption what you want the model to be able to change. Leave out what you want it to absorb as identity.

So: "photo of sks person, wearing a blue shirt, standing in a kitchen, smiling, side lighting". Because I described the shirt and the kitchen, the model attaches those to those words and learns that they vary. Because I never described the face, the face is the residual — the thing that's always there and never named, which attaches to sks person.

If I caption every photo "photo of sks person" and nothing else, the shirt and the kitchen have nowhere to go, so they get absorbed into the token too. That's precisely the overfitting failure people describe as "my LoRA only makes one picture".

Inference settings that matter as much as training

  • LoRA scale 0.6–0.9, not 1.0. Dialling it down often buys a lot of prompt flexibility for very little likeness. This is free tuning after training is done and I suspect it's under-explored.
  • CFG 4–7 for photorealistic faces. SD 1.5's default 7.5 is already on the edge of plastic for skin.
  • DPM++ 2M Karras, 20–30 steps as the quality baseline — before any distillation.
  • Negative prompts carry real weight on SD 1.5 in a way they don't on newer models. Since CFG is computing cond - uncond, putting terms in the negative literally steers the subtraction.
  • Generate at 512 and upscale. Asking SD 1.5 for 1024 directly gives duplicated features and two-headed compositions, because it never saw that resolution in training.
Face restoration will lie to you

GFPGAN and CodeFormer make faces look dramatically cleaner, and they do it by pulling the face toward their learned prior of what a face is. Which means they can smooth away the exact idiosyncrasies that made it recognisably me. It'll look better and score worse. That's a genuine tradeoff and I want the identity number on both sides of it before deciding.

04How I know it worked

Everything above is unfalsifiable without this section. And it matters twice: once for choosing a training recipe, and again — much more — when I start quantizing, because "did 4-bit break it?" has to have an answer that isn't me squinting at pictures.

Two axes, and they trade against each other

Identity. Run a face-recognition model over the generated image and over my real photos, take the cosine similarity of the embeddings. ArcFace via insightface is the standard. Same-person pairs typically land far above different-person pairs, and the gap is wide enough to be a usable signal.

Prompt adherence. CLIP score — cosine similarity between the CLIP embedding of the generated image and of the prompt text. Crude, but it moves in the right direction when the model stops listening.

The reason both are needed: a LoRA that's memorised my selfies scores brilliantly on identity. It just ignores every prompt. Either number alone can be gamed by a broken model, and the pair can't.

A trap I want to name before I fall into it

If I measure identity with ArcFace and then tune everything to maximise ArcFace cosine, I'm optimising against ArcFace's opinion of what makes a face that face. That isn't the same as my mother recognising me. It's still the best instrument I have and I'm going to use it — but a number going up is evidence, not proof, and I should look at a grid of actual images at every decision point.

The fixed prompt set

The thing that makes any of this comparable. Write ~12 prompts once, freeze the seeds, and every checkpoint and every quantization level generates exactly those. Something like:

# identity under variation — should all still be me
"corporate headshot of sks person, navy suit, grey backdrop, softbox lighting"
"sks person, outdoors, overcast daylight, smiling"
"profile view of sks person, three-quarter angle"
"sks person wearing glasses, office background"

# flexibility — does it still obey anything at all
"sks person as an astronaut, spacesuit, on the moon"
"oil painting portrait of sks person in renaissance style"
"sks person in a red hoodie on a beach at sunset"

# the target use case — compliance
"passport photo of sks person, plain white background, neutral expression, front facing"

If the astronaut prompt returns me in my kitchen, the LoRA is overcooked, and I'll know that in thirty seconds instead of three days.

The objective axis: compliance checks

This is the part that makes the whole project measurable in a way most generative work isn't. ID photo standards are written down (ICAO 9303 is the international one), and a useful subset is plain computer vision:

  • Head height ratio — chin to crown as a fraction of frame height, from face landmarks.
  • Eye line position — eyes should sit in a specified band from the bottom.
  • Horizontal centring and head roll angle.
  • Background uniformity — pixel variance in the border region after masking the subject.
  • Shadow detection — luminance asymmetry between left and right halves of the face.
  • Sharpness — variance of the Laplacian.
  • Eyes open, mouth closed, facing forward — landmark geometry.

MediaPipe Face Mesh gives me the landmarks. Under a hundred lines gets a pass-rate percentage that's genuinely objective. And the beauty of it is that quantization damage shows up here differently than it does on identity — a broken VAE decoder tanks sharpness and background uniformity while leaving face geometry intact. Two instruments, two failure signatures.

What not to bother with

FID is the default metric in every image-generation paper and it's useless here. It compares distributions and needs thousands of samples to mean anything, and I care about whether this specific image is this specific person. Wrong tool.

05Getting it onto the phone

This is where the JD-shaped skills actually live, and where most people abandon the project.

Why conversion exists at all

PyTorch is Python. A phone doesn't run Python — it runs a compiled graph on a fixed-function accelerator. So something has to turn a program with branches and dynamic shapes into a static list of ops with known tensor sizes.

Two ways to get there, and they fail differently:

  • Tracing runs the model once with a dummy input and records the ops that actually executed. Simple, works most of the time, and silently bakes in whatever your control flow happened to do on that one input. An if that depended on the data is now permanently resolved to one branch and nothing warns you.
  • Scripting parses the Python directly. More faithful, far pickier about what it accepts.

What breaks, roughly in order of frequency: dynamic shapes, Python control flow that depends on tensor values, einsum and fancy indexing, and ops that simply have no equivalent in the target runtime.

ONNX vs Core ML — which and when

ONNX is the neutral interchange format, and ONNX Runtime executes it on CPU, CUDA, TensorRT and mobile. Core ML is Apple's own format and the only way to touch the Neural Engine.

For an iPhone target, coremltools converting from PyTorch directly is the shorter path — going through ONNX first adds a hop and a set of translation bugs. I'd learn ONNX anyway because it's where the TensorRT and Android paths start, but for this project Core ML is the destination.

The thing that makes this tractable

apple/ml-stable-diffusion is a working, Apple-maintained conversion of Stable Diffusion to Core ML, including the quantization work and a Swift inference package. I'm going to do the conversion myself to hit the failure modes on purpose — that's the point — but having a known-good implementation to diff against when my UNet outputs grey soup removes the single biggest time sink on this project.

What the Neural Engine actually is

Not a small GPU. It's a fixed-function matrix-multiply engine with its own memory layout and a narrow list of supported ops. Three consequences:

  1. Unsupported ops don't fail, they fall back. The tensor gets handed to GPU or CPU mid-graph, and the handoff costs more than the op did.
  2. A model that's "90% on ANE" can be slower than one that's 0% on ANE, if that 10% is scattered through the middle causing constant round trips. Contiguity matters more than percentage.
  3. It wants a specific data layout. Apple's own writeup on deploying transformers to ANE is the essential read: prefer 4D tensors in (B, C, 1, S), split attention heads into separate convolutions, avoid anything that forces a transpose.

Xcode's Core ML performance report shows per-op placement — ANE, GPU or CPU, op by op. That report is the profiling tool for this half of the project.

And MLComputeUnits is a real experiment, not a setting to leave on default: .all, .cpuAndGPU, .cpuAndNeuralEngine, .cpuOnly. The best choice differs per submodel — it's entirely plausible the UNet wants ANE while the VAE decoder is faster on GPU.

Quantization and palettization are not the same thing

These get used interchangeably and conflating them cost me half an hour of confusion, so:

Quantization

Map a float range onto integers with a scale and a zero point. Weights land on a uniform grid. Arithmetic can happen in int8 where the hardware supports it, so you get speed and size.

Palettization

Cluster the weights into a small lookup table — 16 distinct values for 4-bit — and store an index per weight. Not a uniform grid; pointers into a codebook. Mostly a size win. This is what Core ML leans on for diffusion models, and it's why 4-bit SD holds up much better than the phrase "4-bit" suggests.

Calibration, and why activations are the hard part

Weights you can quantize by inspection — they're sitting right there and you can measure their range. Activations you can't, because their range depends on the input. So you run a few hundred representative inputs through, record the observed range at each layer, and derive scales from that. That set is the calibration data, and choosing it badly is one of the standard ways to ship a quantized model that's subtly broken.

Why diffusion UNets resist INT8 specifically

They have activation outliers — a handful of channels whose range is orders of magnitude larger than everything around them. Per-tensor scaling then spends nearly all 256 of its levels covering those few channels, and the rest of the tensor gets crushed into a couple of levels.

Fixes, in order: per-channel scaling instead of per-tensor; then keeping the worst layers in FP16 (mixed precision); then smoothing techniques that shift the difficulty from activations into weights, where it's easier to handle.

Quantize the three networks separately

They have different sensitivities and lumping them together loses information. The VAE decoder in particular is notorious — it's the last thing to touch the image, so its errors land directly in output pixels with nothing downstream to absorb them. Expect to keep it at higher precision than the UNet, and expect the compliance checks to be what tells you so.

Peak memory is a different number from model size

The one that'll bite. A 600 MB model does not use 600 MB at runtime — it uses weights plus activations plus whatever attention allocates at that resolution, and the VAE decoder's peak happens at full 512×512 resolution with wide channel counts. iOS hands an app a memory budget and kills it without ceremony when it's exceeded. So the number I instrument for is peak resident memory, not file size, and they can differ by more than 2×.

06Making it fast enough

Ranked by how much they actually buy, which is not the order people usually try them in.

1

Cut the step count

Biggest single lever, by a long way

20 steps × 2 (CFG) = 40 UNet passes. Distilled models do the whole image in 4–8 steps total, and some in one. That's a 5–10× cut before touching a single weight. Everything else on this list is rounding error by comparison.

LCM-LoRA is the one to reach for first, because the distillation is itself packaged as a LoRA. So it stacks onto the same base model alongside my identity LoRA — no separate checkpoint, no separate conversion. Latent Consistency Models learn to jump directly to the solution of the ODE trajectory instead of walking it. SDXL-Turbo and the adversarial-distillation family go further, to 1–4 steps, at some cost in diversity.

Distilled models also typically run at CFG 1 — guidance is baked in — which removes the 2× on top. Both wins at once.

2

Get the UNet resident on ANE

Large, and the fiddliest to achieve

Correct tensor layout, attention reshaped the way Apple's writeup describes, no ops causing fallback in the middle of the graph. Measured in Xcode's performance report, not assumed.

3

Quantize

Mostly a size and memory win

FP16 is nearly free. 8-bit palettization is usually safe. 4-bit is where it gets interesting and where my eval harness starts earning its keep. Speed gains depend on whether the hardware has a fast path for that precision — don't assume smaller means faster.

4

Batch the CFG pair

Only if not using a distilled model

Run conditional and unconditional as a batch of 2 in one forward pass instead of two sequential passes. Better hardware utilisation for the same arithmetic.

5

Generate small, upscale after

Structural, and it compounds

Diffusion cost scales with latent area. 512² then a cheap 2× upscaler beats 1024² diffusion on both time and memory, and on SD 1.5 it also avoids the duplicated-feature failure at off-native resolutions.

The thermal thing nobody measures

One generation tells me almost nothing about what happens on the tenth. Phones throttle hard under sustained compute, and a model that hits 3 seconds cold can be at 6 seconds after a few minutes of continuous use. For a product where someone generates twenty headshots in a row, the sustained number is the real number. I want a script that runs 50 back to back and logs latency and thermal state per generation, and I want that curve early rather than as a nasty surprise.

07The order to learn this in

Sequenced so that every stage produces something I can look at, and so that I never depend on understanding something I haven't built yet.

0

Write a diffusion model from scratch

One weekend · ~200 lines · runs on the Mac

DDPM on MNIST or CIFAR at 32×32. Noise schedule, a small UNet, the training loop, the sampler. Then implement DDIM on top and watch the step count drop without retraining.

Skippable in theory. I don't want to skip it — it's the difference between using these systems and understanding them, and everything afterwards stops being cargo-culted. Cheapest understanding on the whole list.

1

Drive SD 1.5 through diffusers, then take the pipeline apart

An evening or two

Generate some images. Then rewrite the pipeline call as an explicit loop: encode the prompt, make the latent, step the scheduler by hand, call the UNet twice for CFG, decode. Print tensor shapes at every stage. Swap samplers, sweep CFG from 1 to 15 and watch it go plastic.

This is the step that converts section 01 from words into something I can debug.

2

Build the eval harness — before training anything

One day

ArcFace identity similarity, CLIP prompt score, the compliance checks, the fixed prompt set, and a contact-sheet renderer that lays out a grid with scores under each image.

Doing this first is the highest-leverage ordering choice available. Every experiment after this produces a number instead of an impression, and I never have to re-run anything because I forgot to measure it.

3

Train the first LoRA

One weekend, mostly on the dataset

20 photos, captioned by the rule in section 03. Train on a rented GPU. Checkpoint every 250 steps, then run the harness over every checkpoint and plot identity against flexibility. Pick by the curve.

Then deliberately break it: train to 6000 steps and see what overcooking looks like. Caption everything as just "photo of sks person" and watch the background get baked in. Knowing the failure signatures by sight is worth the GPU minutes.

4

Sweep the axes that matter

A weekend of GPU time, mostly unattended

Rank {8, 16, 32, 64}. LR {5e-5, 1e-4, 2e-4}. Text encoder on/off. Dataset size {10, 20, 30}. Each run scored by the harness. This is where the intuition actually forms, and it's cheap — SD 1.5 LoRAs train in tens of minutes.

5

Add IP-Adapter-FaceID and ControlNet

A weekend

Score zero-shot FaceID against the trained LoRA on the same harness — that's the comparison the whole project is organised around. Then stack them and see whether the combination beats either. ControlNet for framing, which is what makes the compliance numbers move.

6

Convert to Core ML

Two to three weekends · budget more than feels reasonable

Fold the LoRA into the base weights first — that's what makes this tractable at all. Convert each of the three networks separately. Get FP16 working end to end on the Mac before attempting anything clever. Then open Xcode's performance report and find out where the ops actually landed.

Expect this stage to be 80% debugging conversion errors and 20% everything else.

7

Quantize, with the harness running

One weekend

FP16 → 8-bit → 6-bit → 4-bit, per network, scoring identity and compliance at every level. The output is a table: size, peak memory, latency, identity, compliance. That table is the deliverable — it answers "how small can this get before it stops being me", which is the actual question underneath this entire project.

8

Add LCM-LoRA and measure sustained load

A few days

Step count down to 4–8, CFG to 1. Re-score — distillation costs some quality and I want to know how much. Then run 50 generations back to back and plot the thermal curve.

09Open questions

Things I genuinely don't know the answer to, written down now so I notice when I find out.

  • How far can the UNet be quantized before the face stops being mine? The whole project narrows to this. 8-bit is probably fine. 4-bit palettized is the interesting case, and I suspect identity degrades before general image quality does — a face is a high-frequency, low-tolerance thing and a slightly-wrong nose is much more visible than slightly-wrong fabric. Untested suspicion.
  • Does ArcFace cosine track human recognition? It's my primary instrument and I'm taking its validity on faith. At some point I should check a handful of outputs against people who know me and see whether the ranking agrees. If it doesn't, a chunk of section 04 needs rethinking.
  • Can the LoRA be trained on the device at all? Core ML is built for inference. MLX does train and runs on iOS. A per-user LoRA trained entirely on the phone would make the privacy story airtight instead of merely good, and I have no idea whether it's an afternoon or a wall.
  • Does LCM distillation damage identity more than it damages general quality? Four steps is much less room to converge on a specific face. If distillation costs more identity than quantization does, the whole optimisation ordering changes.
  • Do the three networks want different compute units? Plausible that the UNet is fastest on ANE while the VAE decoder is fastest on GPU, in which case the pipeline is heterogeneous and I'll be paying handoff costs between them. No idea until I measure.
  • Is LoRA-plus-FaceID actually better than either alone, or do they fight? Both are steering identity through cross-attention by different routes. Stacking them might compound or might produce something averaged and generic.

10Extensions — going further

Things that aren't in the main plan but are worth doing once it works. The first three make the results I get from section 07 more trustworthy. The rest are what I'd change if this stopped being a learning project and became something people actually use.

Making the measurements honest

A

Score identity with a different face model than the one doing the conditioning

An afternoon · slot into step 2

IP-Adapter-FaceID conditions on an ArcFace embedding. If I then score it with the same ArcFace, the zero-shot route is being graded by the model it was optimised to please, and the LoRA-vs-FaceID comparison in step 5 is tilted before it starts. Score with a second recognizer — a different backbone or training set (AdaFace, FaceNet) — and keep the ArcFace number alongside it. If the two disagree, that disagreement is the interesting result.

B

Test on more faces than mine

A few evenings collecting photos · reuses the whole harness

With one identity I can't tell whether an effect is real or just something about my face. Five to ten people — friends who agree to it, or public-domain portrait sets — varied across skin tone, age, beards and glasses. SD 1.5 and the face encoders both have known demographic gaps, and a single face will never show them to me.

C

Measure the quality ceiling before touching Core ML

A weekend of GPU time · between steps 5 and 6

Run the best pipeline at full precision — FP32/FP16, 30 steps, no distillation, no quantization — on every test identity. That's the best the phone version can ever be. Then every later stage (conversion, 8/6/4-bit, LCM) gets reported as a drop from this ceiling, one change at a time. That separates "the model isn't good enough" from "the compression broke it", which otherwise look identical in a contact sheet.

If this ever becomes a product

The LoRA path doesn't survive contact with real users

Folding a LoRA into the weights works for one face converted once on a Mac. For many users it means re-running coremltools per person — which can't run on iOS — and shipping a separate ~1 GB compiled UNet to each of them. And training on a rented GPU means the selfies leave the phone, which removes the reason to be on-device at all. For a real product, on-device realistically means zero-shot identity, or the approach below.

D

Edit, don't generate

A few weekends · a second pipeline to compare on the harness

A headshot is mostly my real face with new lighting, background, clothes and framing — and the selfie already has the face pixels. So: segment face and hair, inpaint everything else (suit, backdrop, shoulders) with SD 1.5 inpainting plus ControlNet for framing, then a low-strength img2img pass (~0.2–0.35) over the whole image so the lighting on the face matches the new scene.

Identity is preserved by construction rather than approximated. Low-strength img2img only runs a fraction of the denoising steps, so it's cheaper before any distillation. And quantization damage lands mostly on fabric and backdrop, where it's tolerable, instead of on the nose. The costs: pose and expression are locked to the input selfie, wide-angle lens distortion carries through, and lighting mismatch becomes the new hard problem.

E

Try LoRA training on the device itself

Open-ended · could be an afternoon, could be a wall

The real answer to open question 3. MLX on an M-series Mac first, with gradient checkpointing and a low rank, and measure time, peak memory and thermal state per step. Only if that looks survivable, try it on an iPhone. Even a negative result — "it takes 90 minutes and throttles to half speed" — is worth writing up.

F

Benchmark a newer compact base model against SD 1.5

A weekend

SD 1.5 is the right model to learn on — small, documented, supported by apple/ml-stable-diffusion. It isn't the ceiling for on-device image quality in 2026. Compact models built for phones (Snap's SnapGen, NVIDIA's SANA) and photoreal SD 1.5 fine-tunes are worth running through the same harness. Expect worse tooling: fewer adapters, no Core ML reference to diff against.

Two things to settle before shipping anything

Licensing. The pretrained insightface/ArcFace models are released for non-commercial research only, and IP-Adapter-FaceID and InstantID both depend on them. Fine for learning; a product needs a commercially licensed face encoder or one I train myself.

ID photos. Passport authorities require an unaltered photograph of the person. A diffusion-generated face isn't that, however compliant its geometry. The compliance checks are a great measurement instrument; for real ID photos the product would have to be the edit pipeline — background replacement and cropping, face untouched — not generation.
